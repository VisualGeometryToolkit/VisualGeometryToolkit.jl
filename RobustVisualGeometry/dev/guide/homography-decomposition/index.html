Homography Decomposition

$H \longrightarrow (R, \boldsymbol{t}, \boldsymbol{n})$ (Faugeras–Lustman).

1. The Parametric Form

For two pinhole cameras observing a world plane $\pi$ with unit normal $\boldsymbol{n}$ (in camera 1's frame) and signed distance $d > 0$ from camera 1's center, the plane-induced homography is

\[H \;=\; K_2 \!\left(R + \tfrac{1}{d}\, \boldsymbol{t} \boldsymbol{n}^{\mathsf T}\right) K_1^{-1}, \tag{1}\]

where $(R, \boldsymbol{t})$ is the relative pose (camera 1 $\to$ camera 2) and $K_1, K_2$ are intrinsics. Our goal is to invert this relation: given $H$, recover $(R, \boldsymbol{t}, \boldsymbol{n})$.

Calibrated form

Strip out the intrinsics:

\[\boxed{\;\tilde{H} \;=\; K_2^{-1} H K_1 \;=\; R + \frac{1}{d}\,\boldsymbol{t} \boldsymbol{n}^{\mathsf T}.\;} \tag{2}\]

All of the decomposition math operates on $\tilde{H}$. This requires calibrated cameras; if $K_1, K_2$ are unknown, the decomposition is not well-defined.

Degree-of-freedom count

$\tilde{H}$ is projective, so 8 DOF. On the right-hand side of (2), $R$ contributes 3, $\boldsymbol{t}$ contributes 3, $\boldsymbol{n}$ (unit vector) contributes 2, and $d$ contributes 1, for 9 DOF; but the product $\boldsymbol{t} \boldsymbol{n}^{\mathsf T} / d$ is invariant under $(\boldsymbol{t}, d) \mapsto (\lambda \boldsymbol{t}, \lambda d)$, absorbing one DOF and bringing the count to 8. So decomposition is generically well-posed up to a finite set of sign ambiguities.

2. Faugeras–Lustman Algorithm

This is the classical algorithm (also the one OpenCV's decomposeHomographyMat and PoseLib's motion_from_homography use).

Step 1: SVD and middle-singular-value normalization

Compute

\[\tilde{H} = U\,\Sigma\,V^{\mathsf T}, \qquad \Sigma = \mathrm{diag}(d_1, d_2, d_3), \qquad d_1 \ge d_2 \ge d_3 > 0. \tag{3}\]

Normalize so the middle singular value equals 1:

\[\tilde{H} \leftarrow \tilde{H} / d_2, \qquad d_1 \leftarrow d_1/d_2, \quad d_3 \leftarrow d_3/d_2. \tag{4}\]

After this, $d'_1 \ge 1 \ge d'_3$.

Step 2: reduce to the SVD basis

In the coordinate system defined by $V$ (where the normalized plane normal aligns with the axes), write

\[R = U R' V^{\mathsf T}, \qquad \boldsymbol{t} = U \boldsymbol{t}', \qquad \boldsymbol{n} = V \boldsymbol{n}'. \tag{5}\]

Substituting into (2) and using $U^{\mathsf T} U = V^{\mathsf T} V = I$, the problem becomes: find $R'$, $\boldsymbol{t}'$, $\boldsymbol{n}'$ such that

\[\Sigma' = R' + \boldsymbol{t}' \boldsymbol{n}'^{\,\mathsf{T}}, \qquad \Sigma' = \mathrm{diag}(d'_1, 1, d'_3). \tag{6}\]

Step 3: closed-form expressions for n′

Under the generic case $d'_1 > 1 > d'_3$, one can show (Faugeras–Lustman

  1. that the normal has the form

\[\boldsymbol{n}' = \bigl(x_1,\; 0,\; x_3\bigr)^{\mathsf T}, \tag{7}\]

with

\[x_1 \;=\; \pm\sqrt{\frac{d'_1{}^{\,2} - 1}{d'_1{}^{\,2} - d'_3{}^{\,2}}}, \qquad x_3 \;=\; \pm\sqrt{\frac{1 - d'_3{}^{\,2}}{d'_1{}^{\,2} - d'_3{}^{\,2}}}. \tag{8}\]

The two sign choices $\epsilon_1, \epsilon_3 \in \{+1, -1\}$ yield four candidate solutions. The middle component is zero because the plane normal has no component in the direction of the middle singular vector (the direction in which the scene's out-of-plane depth has no effect).

Step 4: recover R′ and t′ per candidate

Let

\[\sin\theta = \frac{(d'_1 - d'_3)\, x_1\, x_3}{1}, \tag{9}\]

\[\cos\theta = \frac{d'_1\, x_3^{\,2} + d'_3\, x_1^{\,2}}{1}. \tag{10}\]

Then the rotation in the reduced basis is

\[R' = \begin{pmatrix} \cos\theta & 0 & -\sin\theta \\ 0 & 1 & 0 \\ \sin\theta & 0 & \cos\theta \end{pmatrix}, \tag{11}\]

and the translation is

\[\boldsymbol{t}' = \bigl(d'_1 - d'_3\bigr)\, \bigl(x_1,\; 0,\; -x_3\bigr)^{\mathsf T}. \tag{12}\]

With the two independent sign choices for $(x_1, x_3)$, this yields four distinct $(R', \boldsymbol{t}', \boldsymbol{n}')$ tuples.

Step 5: transform back to the original frame

For each candidate,

\[R = U R' V^{\mathsf T}, \qquad \boldsymbol{t} = U\,\boldsymbol{t}', \qquad \boldsymbol{n} = V\,\boldsymbol{n}'. \tag{13}\]

Step 6: cheirality selection

Of the four candidates, typically only one (occasionally two symmetric ones) corresponds to a physically valid configuration where both cameras view the plane from the same side. The valid candidates satisfy

\[\boldsymbol{n}^{\mathsf T} X_i > 0 \qquad \text{for all correspondences } X_i \text{ on the plane, in cam 1's frame}. \tag{14}\]

Equivalently, for each candidate, every sampled correspondence must produce a positive projective scale factor $\lambda_i$ in $\mathbf{x}'_i = \lambda_i H \mathbf{x}_i$. The two or so candidates that satisfy this test are the physically meaningful decompositions.

In the benchmark setting where a ground-truth pose $(R_{gt}, \boldsymbol{t}_{gt})$ is known, one can instead pick the candidate minimizing

\[e_R(R, R_{gt}) + \alpha\, e_T(\boldsymbol{t}, \boldsymbol{t}_{gt}) \tag{15}\]

directly. This is what HEB and Shekhovtsov do when computing pose errors.

3. Malis–Vargas Algorithm (OpenCV / PoseLib / Shekhovtsov)

An alternative decomposition, used by cv2.decomposeHomographyMat and PoseLib's motion_from_homography, is based on eigendecomposition of $\tilde{H}^{\mathsf T} \tilde{H}$ rather than direct SVD analysis. It produces the same four candidate tuples as Faugeras–Lustman but through a different computational path. Shekhovtsov's PyTorch port follows this algorithm exactly.

Step 1: sign normalization and middle-singular-value scaling

If $\det \tilde{H} < 0$, flip $\tilde{H} \leftarrow -\tilde{H}$ (homographies are defined up to scalar multiplication, so this is a free choice for orientation consistency). Then

\[\tilde{H} = U \Sigma V^{\mathsf T},\qquad H_2 = \tilde{H} / s_2, \tag{16}\]

where $s_2$ is the middle singular value. After this, $H_2$ has singular values $(s_1, 1, s_3)$ with $s_1 \ge 1 \ge s_3$.

Step 2: eigendecomposition of H₂ᵀH₂

Compute

\[H_2^{\mathsf T} H_2 = V \Sigma^2 V^{\mathsf T}, \qquad \Sigma^2 = \mathrm{diag}(s_1^2, s_2^2, s_3^2). \tag{17}\]

Shekhovtsov's PyTorch port computes this with torch.linalg.eigh (symmetric eigendecomposition), which returns eigenvalues in ascending order, and extracts

\[s_1^2 = \text{evals}[2],\quad s_2^2 = \text{evals}[1],\quad s_3^2 = \text{evals}[0],\qquad v_k = \text{evecs}[:,k]. \tag{18}\]

Step 3: construct twin vectors in the v₁–v₃ plane

Define

\[\boxed{\;u_{1,2} \;=\; \frac{\sqrt{1 - s_3^2}\, v_1 \;\pm\; \sqrt{s_1^2 - 1}\, v_3}{\sqrt{s_1^2 - s_3^2}}\;} \tag{19}\]

The $\pm$ sign gives two candidate unit vectors in the $(v_1, v_3)$ plane. These play the role of the sign-ambiguous $x_1, x_3$ in Faugeras–Lustman.

Step 4: orthonormal bases via cross-products

For each $u_i \in \{u_1, u_2\}$, build two orthonormal triads:

\[U_i = \bigl[\, v_2,\quad u_i,\quad v_2 \times u_i\, \bigr], \tag{20}\]

\[W_i = \bigl[\, H_2\, v_2,\quad H_2\, u_i,\quad (H_2\, v_2) \times (H_2\, u_i)\, \bigr]. \tag{21}\]

$U_i$ is the source frame; $W_i$ is the image of $U_i$'s first two columns under $H_2$, with the third column taken as the cross product (forcing it to be a right-handed orthonormal triad).

Step 5: rotation via orthogonal Procrustes

Each column of $U_i$ maps to the corresponding column of $W_i$ under the rotation, so

\[\boxed{\;R_i = W_i\, U_i^{\mathsf T}\;} \tag{22}\]

is the orthogonal Procrustes solution (since both $U_i$ and $W_i$ are orthonormal, $W_i U_i^{\mathsf T}$ is itself a rotation).

Step 6: plane normal and translation

\[n_i = v_2 \times u_i, \qquad \text{flipped so } (n_i)_3 \ge 0, \tag{23}\]

\[t_i = (H_2 - R_i)\, n_i. \tag{24}\]

The translation follows from the identity $H_2 = R + t\, n^{\mathsf T} \;\Longrightarrow\; (H_2 - R)\, n = t\, n^{\mathsf T} n = t$ (since $\|n\|=1$).

Step 7: four candidate solutions

Because $(R, t, n) \mapsto (R, -t, -n)$ yields the same $H$ (see Section 5), each of the two rotations $R_1, R_2$ produces two valid pairings:

Solution #$R$$t$$n$
0$R_1$$+t_1$$+n_1$
1$R_1$$-t_1$$-n_1$
2$R_2$$+t_2$$+n_2$
3$R_2$$-t_2$$-n_2$

These are the four candidate decompositions returned by cv2.decomposeHomographyMat, PoseLib's motion_from_homography, and Shekhovtsov's port.

Comparison to Faugeras–Lustman

Both algorithms produce the same four candidates up to numerical precision. Differences:

  • Faugeras–Lustman: operates directly on the SVD of $\tilde{H}$, closed-form expressions for $x_1, x_3$. Requires case analysis for degenerate singular-value configurations.
  • Malis–Vargas: operates on the symmetric matrix $\tilde{H}^{\mathsf T} \tilde{H}$ via eigh. Numerically more stable for near-symmetric homographies. The Procrustes step $R = W U^{\mathsf T}$ handles sign conventions implicitly.

OpenCV chose Malis–Vargas; since PoseLib and Shekhovtsov both adopt the same algorithm, it is the de-facto standard for reproducibility.

4. Degenerate Cases

Equation (8) is well-defined only in the generic case $d'_1 > 1 > d'_3$. Special cases:

$d_1 = d_2 = d_3$ (pure rotation). The homography reduces to $H = K_2 R K_1^{-1}$, i.e., $\boldsymbol{t} = \boldsymbol{0}$. The plane normal is undetermined. Return only the rotation.

$d_1 = d_2 > d_3$ or $d_1 > d_2 = d_3$. Two singular values coincide. The decomposition is still valid but has a continuous family of solutions along one axis. Fix one representative by convention; in practice this case is vanishingly rare for realistic data.

$d_3 \approx 0$. The homography is near-rank-deficient, i.e., the minimal solver or sample was degenerate. Decomposition becomes numerically unreliable and downstream sample-validity checks (conditioning, cheirality) would have already rejected the model.

5. Role of the Plane Normal

The plane normal $\boldsymbol{n}$ is an output of the decomposition, not an input. The 4-fold sign ambiguity lives in $(\boldsymbol{n}, \boldsymbol{t})$: flipping both $\boldsymbol{n}$ and $\boldsymbol{t}$ yields the same $H$ (same plane seen "from the other side" with opposite pose convention). Physical validity (cheirality) fixes which of the four algebraic solutions is real.

For pose-error measurement we only need $(R, \boldsymbol{t})$; $\boldsymbol{n}$ is a disambiguating byproduct and does not appear in the final $(e_R, e_T)$ metric. It becomes useful if downstream reasoning wants to know which plane the homography corresponds to — e.g., to distinguish front wall from ground plane in a scene with multiple planes.
